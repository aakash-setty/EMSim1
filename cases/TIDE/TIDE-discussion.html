Learning goals

  1. Recognize severe pneumonia with sepsis and poor perfusion.
  2. Step oxygen up to high flow, and do not read a better saturation as a better patient.
  3. Give a beta-lactam and a macrolide within the hour.
  4. Give fluid, reassess, and start norepinephrine when the mean drifts back under 65.
  5. Intubate when noninvasive support is failing, before he crashes.
  6. Have a vasopressor running and preoxygenate with positive pressure before induction.

The patient

Pneumonia is the easy part. The hard part is that his breathing and his circulation both fail, and the monitor hides it.

58-year-old man. Four days of fever and shaking chills, rusty sputum and right-sided pleuritic pain, breathless since last night. Temperature 39.2, heart rate 118, pressure 104/60, breathing 32, saturation 85% on room air. Lactate 3.8, opacities in both lower zones, urine pneumococcal antigen positive.

1. Is it severe pneumonia?

  • Severe means one major criterion or three minor ones. The major ones are septic shock needing a vasopressor and respiratory failure needing a ventilator. He reaches both before the end of the case.
  • He meets four minor criteria on arrival. That is enough before his pressure falls. Severe pneumonia goes to intensive care and changes the antibiotic.
  • His lactate of 3.8 is poor perfusion while his pressure is still 104/60. Treat it as sepsis with hypoperfusion from the start.

2. A better number is not a better patient

  • High flow is a good first device. It is as good as BiPAP for most patients with hypoxemia who are not immunocompromised. [UNVERIFIED: RENOVATE, JAMA 2025, read through summaries]
  • Judge support by the work of breathing. A rising rate, accessory muscles, paradoxical breathing, confusion and a rising carbon dioxide all mean failure, whatever the saturation.
  • The ROX index is saturation over FiO2, divided by the breathing rate. Low values predict failure of high flow. It was validated at 2, 6 and 12 hours, and his falls from 5.1 to 2.3. [UNVERIFIED: Roca et al., AJRCCM 2019, from memory]
  • Waiting costs. Intubating late after failed high flow is associated with worse outcomes in observational data. [UNVERIFIED: from the owner's structure]

3. Fluid, then norepinephrine

  • About 30 mL/kg of crystalloid is suggested, with low certainty. In obesity, use an ideal or adjusted weight. His predicted weight is about 73 kg. [UNVERIFIED: Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2026, read through summaries]
  • A response that fades has stopped working. More fluid moves the number for a few minutes. In this case a third bolus adds water to the lungs.
  • Target a mean of 65 with norepinephrine. A good peripheral line is enough to start it. Do not wait for a central line.
  • Hydrocortisone is suggested once he needs a vasopressor. In severe pneumonia without shock the ATS 2025 update suggests a steroid too, though not in influenza. CAPE COD, which showed benefit, excluded septic shock. [UNVERIFIED: read through summaries]

4. Antibiotics

  • A beta-lactam plus a macrolide, such as ceftriaxone and azithromycin. A beta-lactam plus a respiratory fluoroquinolone is the alternative. A fluoroquinolone alone is not enough in severe pneumonia. [UNVERIFIED: IDSA/ATS 2019, from memory]
  • Within the hour in shock. Take blood cultures first if that does not delay the drug.
  • He has no risk for MRSA or Pseudomonas: no recent antibiotics, no hospital stay, no prior culture. Broader cover adds harm without benefit.
  • The antibiotic changes nothing in the case's minutes. It changes the next days. The case scores it and does not show it.

5. Intubating a patient in shock

  • Resuscitate before you intubate. Induction and positive pressure take away the tone and the venous return he is living on. Have the drip running first.
  • Preoxygenate with positive pressure. BiPAP is preferred to a facemask. High flow is the fallback. [UNVERIFIED: ACEP clinical policy, read through its web page, and PREOXI, NEJM 2024, not read]
  • Choose a hemodynamically gentle induction. The ACEP policy advises against propofol, midazolam or fentanyl for induction when post-intubation hypotension is a risk. A push-dose vasopressor bridges until the drip catches up.
  • The case makes the collapse certain. In real patients it is common, not certain. [UNVERIFIED: model teaching choice, owner decision B4]

6. After the tube, and where he goes

  • Set the ventilator from predicted weight, not actual weight. About 6 mL/kg is near 440 mL for him, not the 570 mL his 95 kg would suggest. Keep the plateau pressure at 30 or less. The case has no ventilator panel.
  • Sedate as soon as the tube is in, above all after rocuronium, which outlasts the induction drug. Awareness with paralysis was reported in 2.6% and 3.4% of emergency patients in two studies. [UNVERIFIED: ED-AWARENESS, Ann Emerg Med 2021, and Fuller, Crit Care Med 2022, read through summaries]
  • On the ventilator his P/F is about 111 (PaO2 78 on FiO2 0.7), in the moderate range of the Berlin definition.
  • Intensive care, with severe pneumonia and septic shock named at handover.
  • The clock was compressed. Failure on high flow develops over hours. He failed because this patient fails, not because of anything you did.

Traps

  • Reading 92% on high flow as success while he is breathing at 30.
  • A third and fourth bolus for a pressure that keeps drifting back.
  • Waiting for a central line to start norepinephrine.
  • Furosemide for crackles that are pneumonia.
  • Intubating with nothing running.
  • Propofol to induce a patient in shock.
  • No sedation after rocuronium.
