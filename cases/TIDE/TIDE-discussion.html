The case

A 58-year-old man with four days of fever, cough and rusty sputum arrives breathing 32 times a minute with a saturation of 85% on room air. He has pneumococcal pneumonia in three lobes. Over the next minutes it takes him into septic shock, and he fails high flow whatever you do. The skill is seeing it and getting him onto a ventilator safely.

The right care, in order:

  1. Oxygen up to high flow. Blood cultures and a lactate, then ceftriaxone and azithromycin within the hour.
  2. A fluid bolus. When the pressure drifts back down, norepinephrine, then hydrocortisone and the ICU.
  3. When he tires, intubate with norepinephrine already running, BiPAP to preoxygenate and a reduced induction dose. Ventilate at about 6 mL/kg of predicted body weight.

The traps here are furosemide, metoprolol, dopamine and a trip to CT.

Learning goals

  1. Recognize sepsis with poor perfusion before the pressure falls.
  2. Give a beta-lactam and a macrolide within the hour.
  3. Know when fluid has done what it can, and start norepinephrine.
  4. Judge high flow by his effort, and intubate before he tires.
  5. Prepare his circulation before the tube goes in.

1. What pneumonia does to him

Pus fills the air sacs, so blood passes them without picking up oxygen and the lung turns stiff and heavy. The inflammation does not stay in the lung. It relaxes and leaks the vessels everywhere.

  • Blood that passes a filled air sac comes back without oxygen. This is shunt, and extra oxygen helps it less than it helps other causes of a low saturation, because the oxygen never reaches that blood.
  • Severe pneumonia means one major criterion, a vasopressor or a ventilator, or three minor ones, such as a rate of 30 or more, a P/F ratio of 250 or less, infiltrates in several lobes, confusion or a urea nitrogen of 20 or more. It usually belongs in intensive care. [UNVERIFIED: IDSA/ATS 2007 criteria, kept in 2019, checked in secondary sources on 4 November 2026]

2. A normal pressure can hide shock

Shock is a failure to deliver the oxygen the tissues need, and blood pressure is not part of the definition. Pressure depends on how hard the heart pumps and how tight the vessels are. Sepsis relaxes the vessels, and the heart speeds up to make up the difference.

  • A normal pressure with a fast heart rate is a pressure being held up. A mean of 75 with a heart rate of 118 and a lactate of 3.8 suggests poor blood flow to the tissues.
  • In sepsis, repeat a lactate over 2. A lactate of 4 or more calls for a full fluid bolus even when the pressure is normal. [UNVERIFIED: Surviving Sepsis hour-1 bundle, 2018, from memory]
  • Give the antibiotic before the pressure falls. In septic shock each hour of delay was linked to lower survival in an observational study. [UNVERIFIED: Kumar et al., Crit Care Med 2006, abstract read only]

3. What fluid can do, and what it cannot

Fluid fills the relaxed vessels for a while, then leaks out of them. It does not tighten them. Norepinephrine does.

  • Give about 30 mL/kg of crystalloid and reassess after each bolus. In obesity, base the dose on an ideal or adjusted weight. [UNVERIFIED: Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2026, read through summaries]
  • A rise that fades within minutes means fluid has done what it can. In pneumonia, more fluid after that mostly adds water to lungs that are already failing.
  • Start norepinephrine when the mean stays under 65 after fluid. A good peripheral line is enough to start it.
  • Add hydrocortisone when shock needs an ongoing vasopressor. [UNVERIFIED: Surviving Sepsis Campaign, from memory]

4. A good saturation can hide failure

The saturation shows the result, not what it costs. The same number can take almost no effort, or nearly all the patient has.

  • Judge breathing support by effort: the rate, the accessory muscles, paradoxical breathing, confusion and the CO2. These often change before the saturation does.
  • With an acid load the CO2 should be low. By Winter's formula it should be 1.5 times the bicarbonate plus 8, give or take 2. A CO2 of 46 against an expected 34 to 38 means breathing is not keeping up.
  • The ROX index, saturation over FiO2 divided by the rate, puts effort and oxygen in one number. On high flow, values under 2.85 at 2 hours, 3.47 at 6 hours or 3.85 at 12 hours predicted failure in a validation cohort. [UNVERIFIED: Roca et al., AJRCCM 2019, abstract and editorial read through search excerpts]
  • In an observational study, intubation delayed after failing high flow was linked to higher mortality. Once support is failing, waiting adds risk. [UNVERIFIED: Kang et al., Intensive Care Med 2015, title read only]

5. Intubating a patient in shock

Three things hold the pressure up in a patient in shock: adrenaline, the pull of their own breathing drawing blood back to the heart, and fast breathing that clears acid. Intubation takes away all three at once.

  • A shock index, heart rate divided by systolic pressure, of 0.8 or more before intubation predicted low pressure after it in a retrospective study. His was 1.1 on arrival. [UNVERIFIED: Heffner et al., J Crit Care 2012, abstract read through a search excerpt]
  • Start the vasopressor before induction, or have a push-dose pressor drawn up, and lower the induction dose.
  • Preoxygenate with BiPAP. In a randomized trial it cut low oxygen during intubation from 18.5% with a face mask to 9.1%. [UNVERIFIED: PREOXI, Gibbs et al., NEJM 2024, abstract read through a search excerpt]

Take home

  • A normal blood pressure does not rule out shock. Read the heart rate, the lactate and the skin together.
  • Severe pneumonia gets a beta-lactam with a macrolide within the hour.
  • When the response to fluid fades and the mean stays under 65, start norepinephrine.
  • Judge breathing support by effort and CO2, and intubate before the patient tires.
  • Start the vasopressor before the induction drug.
