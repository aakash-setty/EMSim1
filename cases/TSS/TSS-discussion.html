Learning goals

  1. See shock in a young woman who is talking and looks well.
  2. In fever, rash and low pressure, ask about periods and tampons, and examine the pelvis.
  3. Take the source out. Nothing given through a vein replaces it.
  4. Give fluid, reassess, and start norepinephrine when the mean stays under 65.
  5. Cover staphylococci including MRSA, and add clindamycin for the toxin.
  6. Admit to intensive care and name the organ injuries at handover.

The patient

She looks well and thinks it is a stomach flu. Her numbers say shock.

22-year-old woman. Two days of fever, vomiting and watery diarrhea, red all over since yesterday, then she fainted in the shower. Temperature 39.4, heart rate 128, pressure 94/58, red eyes, a blanching rash down to her palms. Lactate 4.6.

1. Shock in a patient who compensates

  • A young patient holds her pressure until she cannot. Slow capillary refill, a fast heart rate and a raised lactate are shock already.
  • Do not let a likely story explain away the vital signs. A stomach bug does not cause a fever of 39.4, a rash to the palms and a lactate of 4.6.
  • Vomiting and diarrhea are part of toxic shock. The first CDC report said it "typically begins suddenly with high fever, vomiting, and profuse watery diarrhea". In a French series, 45 of 55 menstrual cases had digestive symptoms. [UNVERIFIED: the French series, eClinicalMedicine 2020, read only through a model's review of 29 October 2026]

2. Shock with no source: keep looking

  • Ask about her period and tampons. She will not bring it up. In this case a question about pregnancy is the opening.
  • Examine everywhere: pelvis, skin, wounds, anything packed or implanted. Then take the source out.
  • About half of staphylococcal toxic shock is menstrual. The rest follows wounds, surgery, packing, burns and skin infection. [UNVERIFIED: from the emDocs review citing Reingold 1982, not read in the original]

3. Fluid, then norepinephrine

  • About 30 mL/kg of crystalloid in the first 3 hours. For her 62 kg that is close to two liters. The guideline calls this a weak suggestion on low-quality evidence.
  • Target a mean of 65. Norepinephrine is the first-line vasopressor.
  • A good peripheral line is enough to start it. Do not wait for a central line.
  • A response that fades is information. More fluid moves the number for a minute. The vasopressor holds it.

4. Is it toxic shock?

  • It is a clinical diagnosis. Fever of 38.9 or more, a diffuse red rash, a systolic of 90 or less, and three or more organ systems. Peeling skin follows one to two weeks later.
  • She meets the pressure criterion only once she drops. At 94 on arrival she does not, and she is still in shock. The definition is for counting cases, not for deciding when to treat.
  • Blood cultures are usually negative in the staphylococcal form, about 5% positive. A negative culture does not argue against it. [UNVERIFIED: from the emDocs review citing Davis 1982 and Reingold 1982, not read in the original]
  • White cells in the urine without bacteria are part of the syndrome, not a urinary infection. Calling them the source ends the search early.

5. Antibiotics

  • Within the hour: vancomycin plus clindamycin. Vancomycin covers Staphylococcus aureus including MRSA. Broad cover such as ceftriaxone is reasonable while the source is unknown.
  • Clindamycin is for the toxin. It stops bacterial protein synthesis and so cuts toxin production, which a cell-wall antibiotic does not. Give it with vancomycin, not instead, because clindamycin resistance in S. aureus is common. Linezolid also suppresses toxin. The evidence is laboratory and observational, and this case does not score it. [UNVERIFIED: no trial in staphylococcal toxic shock]
  • Immunoglobulin can be considered in severe disease that does not respond to support. The evidence is stronger in streptococcal toxic shock. [UNVERIFIED]

6. Where she goes

  • Intensive care. She needs a vasopressor and close watching of the kidneys, the liver and the platelets.
  • Name the organ injuries at handover: kidney injury, liver injury, low platelets, muscle breakdown.
  • Before she leaves the hospital, tell her toxic shock can recur with tampon use. [UNVERIFIED: recurrence counseling as usual practice]

Traps

  • Calling it a stomach bug or a sunburn because she looks well.
  • Reading a pressure of 94 as reassuring in a heart rate of 128.
  • Treating pyuria as the source and stopping the search.
  • Fluid and a vasopressor, and no pelvic exam.
  • Waiting for a central line to start norepinephrine.
  • Sending her home.
