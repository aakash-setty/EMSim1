The case

A 22-year-old woman with two days of fever, vomiting and watery diarrhea and a red rash since yesterday, who fainted in the shower. She talks, apologizes for coming in and calls it a stomach flu. Her heart rate is 128 and her lactate 4.6. A tampon left in for about four days is growing staph that makes a toxin. She has toxic shock, and she keeps sliding until the tampon is out.

The right care, in order:

  1. See the shock behind her story: the heart rate, the cool hands, the slow refill and the lactate. Start fluid and send blood cultures.
  2. Ask about her period and tampons, examine the pelvis and take the tampon out.
  3. Vancomycin and clindamycin within the hour.
  4. When her mean pressure stays under 65 after about 30 mL/kg, start norepinephrine through a peripheral line. Admit her to intensive care.

The traps here are sending her home with gastroenteritis, slowing her heart rate, ibuprofen, a diuretic, a lumbar puncture and intubating early.

Learning goals

  1. See shock in a young woman who is talking and looks well.
  2. In fever, rash and low pressure, ask about periods and tampons, and examine the pelvis.
  3. Take the source out. Nothing given through a vein replaces it.
  4. Give fluid, reassess, and start norepinephrine when the mean stays under 65.
  5. Cover staph including MRSA, and add clindamycin for the toxin.
  6. Admit to intensive care and name the organ injuries at handover.

1. What the toxin does

The toxin made by the staph, most often TSST-1, is a superantigen. It binds T cells outside the usual antigen slot and switches on up to one in five of them at once. The flood of cytokines that follows makes vessels leak and relax everywhere. [UNVERIFIED: up to 20% of T cells, Moza et al., EMBO J 2007, read through a search excerpt]

  • That is why toxic shock looks like more than an infection: high fever, a diffuse red rash that peels a week or two later, red eyes, vomiting, diarrhea and several organs failing at once.
  • The bacteria stay at the source, so blood cultures are usually negative. A negative culture does not argue against it. [UNVERIFIED: about 5% positive, from the emDocs review citing Davis 1982, not read in the original]
  • Most adults carry antibodies to TSST-1. Toxic shock happens in those who do not, which is one reason it strikes young, healthy people. [UNVERIFIED: low or absent antibodies at onset in most menstrual cases, Frontiers in Immunology 2023, read through a search excerpt]

2. What shock is

Shock is a failure of the circulation to deliver the oxygen the tissues need. While delivery is enough, the tissues take what they need. Below a critical point they cannot, and lactate rises. Blood pressure is not part of the definition. [UNVERIFIED: ESICM consensus, Cecconi et al. 2014, definition read through a search excerpt]

  • A normal pressure does not rule out shock. Young patients hold their pressure longest by raising the heart rate and tightening the vessels, so in them the pressure says least. Her 94 systolic came with a heart rate of 128.
  • Look for poor flow together: a fast heart rate, cool skin, slow capillary refill, confusion, little urine and a raised lactate. Each one has other causes, lactate included, so no single sign makes the diagnosis.
  • Septic shock is the one form whose formal definition needs low pressure: a vasopressor to keep the mean at 65 and a lactate over 2 after fluid. That label marks the sickest group. It is not a reason to wait. [UNVERIFIED: Sepsis-3, Singer et al. 2016, criteria read in the abstract]

3. Where each treatment acts

Fluid and norepinephrine act at the end of the chain. They hold the pressure up while the cause goes on. Only removing the source and cutting toxin production stop the chain.

  • In shock with fever and a rash, ask every woman about her period and tampons, and examine the pelvis. Patients rarely raise it. Look also at wounds, surgical sites, nasal packing and burns, where about half of cases start. [UNVERIFIED: from the emDocs review citing Reingold 1982, not read in the original]
  • White cells in the urine with no bacteria are part of the syndrome. Calling it a urine infection ends the search early.
  • Vancomycin covers staph including MRSA. Clindamycin stops the bacteria making protein, so it cuts toxin output, which a cell-wall drug does not. Give both, because clindamycin resistance is common. The evidence is laboratory and observational. [UNVERIFIED: no trial in staphylococcal toxic shock]

4. What fluid can do, and what it cannot

Leaky, relaxed vessels are the problem. Fluid refills them for a while, then leaks out. It does not tighten them. Norepinephrine does.

  • Give about 30 mL/kg of crystalloid, about 1.9 liters for a 62 kg woman, and reassess after each bolus. [UNVERIFIED: Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021, weak recommendation, read in the emDocs summary]
  • A rise that fades within minutes means fluid has done what it can. More fluid after that mostly ends up in the tissues and the lungs.
  • Start norepinephrine when the mean stays under 65 after fluid. A good peripheral line is enough. Do not wait for a central line.

Take home

  • Shock is the tissues not getting the oxygen they need. A normal pressure does not rule it out.
  • In shock with fever and a rash, ask about periods and tampons, and examine the pelvis.
  • Take the source out. Nothing given through a vein replaces it.
  • Add clindamycin to vancomycin to cut toxin production.
  • When the mean stays under 65 after fluid, start norepinephrine through a peripheral line.
