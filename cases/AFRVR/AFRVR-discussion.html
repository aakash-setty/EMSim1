Learning goals

  1. Recognize atrial fibrillation with a rapid ventricular response, and recognize when it arrives together with acute decompensated heart failure.
  2. Look at the ventricle before choosing a rate-control drug. A minute of cardiac ultrasound decides the drug list.
  3. Say why intravenous diltiazem and verapamil are the wrong drugs once systolic function is known to be reduced, even when they lower the rate.
  4. Treat the breathing first. Apply positive pressure early, diurese, and keep fluid out of wet lungs.
  5. Know when synchronized cardioversion is the answer and when it is not, and why the duration of the arrhythmia matters.
  6. Start anticoagulation on a stated risk assessment, and send the patient to a unit that can continue what the department started.

The patient

A 68-year-old man with hypertension, diabetes and non-obstructive coronary disease, no known arrhythmia and no known heart failure. His heart has been racing since the previous afternoon and he has become steadily more breathless. On arrival the rate is about 160 and irregular, the saturation 88% on room air, the respiratory rate 30 and the pressure 132/78. He has crackles, a raised venous pressure, leg edema, and diffuse B-lines on lung ultrasound. Cardiac ultrasound shows global hypokinesis with an ejection fraction of about 30 to 35%.

Two problems are presenting as one. The rate is what you see first. The ventricle is what decides the treatment.

Why a fast rate fails a weak ventricle

The ventricle fills during diastole. As the rate rises, systole shortens only a little and diastole absorbs most of the loss. In sinus rhythm the atrial contraction at the end of diastole tops up the ventricle, and in atrial fibrillation that contribution is gone. A stiff or failing ventricle depends on both more than a healthy one does. The irregular beat adds to the problem, because short cycles fill poorly and are not fully made up by the long ones.

Less filling means a smaller stroke volume and a higher left atrial pressure. That pressure is transmitted to the pulmonary capillaries and the lungs fill with fluid. Hypoxemia and the work of breathing raise sympathetic tone, which drives the ventricular rate higher still. Each turn of the cycle makes the next one worse.

Which came first does not need to be settled in the department. A sustained rapid ventricular response can itself cause a cardiomyopathy that recovers once the rate is controlled, and a dilated left atrium in an existing cardiomyopathy readily fibrillates. The plan treats both halves either way.

Sequencing: breathing first

The number that is easiest to fix is the heart rate. The number that will kill him is the oxygen saturation. Positive pressure recruits flooded alveoli, cuts the work of breathing and lowers preload and left ventricular afterload, and it does so within minutes. Rate control gives the ventricle time to fill. Diuresis removes the excess salt and water, but net removal takes hours. All three are needed, and they are started together, not in turn.

A fluid bolus is the reflex answer to tachycardia, and here it is harmful. The tachycardia is an arrhythmia, not hypovolemia, and the ventricle cannot handle more volume. Fluid goes to the lungs. Lung ultrasound answers the question in under a minute.

Choosing the rate-control agent

For most patients with atrial fibrillation and a rapid ventricular response, an intravenous beta blocker or a non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker is the usual first choice. This patient is the exception. Diltiazem and verapamil reduce contractility. The 2023 ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS guideline gives a harm recommendation against intravenous non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers in patients with known moderate or severe left ventricular systolic dysfunction, with or without decompensated heart failure [UNVERIFIED: class 3 harm per the ACEP Now summary, wording not checked against the guideline text].

Diltiazem given on arrival, before anyone has looked at the heart, is a common and defensible first move. Continuing it after the ultrasound is the error this case exists to teach. The drug does exactly what was asked, and the rate falls, but it is still the wrong drug for this ventricle. Watch the blood pressure.

The target is clinical response, not a number. A resting rate under 110 is the lenient target supported by the RACE II trial, which enrolled patients with permanent atrial fibrillation, not patients in acute decompensation. A patient at a rate of 108 who is still at 86% and breathing 32 times a minute has not been resuscitated.

Correct low magnesium. It is cheap and safe, potassium does not correct properly without it, and small trials suggest it helps rate control when added to nodal blockade [UNVERIFIED: evidence base is small trials and a meta-analysis, per the case note].

Cardioversion

Synchronized cardioversion is the immediate answer when the arrhythmia is causing hemodynamic instability: shock, ischemic chest pain, altered mental status, or acute heart failure that the rate is driving. This patient meets only the last criterion, he is awake with a systolic pressure of 132, and the heart failure may be as much cause as effect. Cardioversion of a rhythm driven by an underlying decompensation also tends not to hold.

Duration matters. He noticed the palpitations about 18 hours ago, but a week of leg swelling before that leaves open the possibility that he was in atrial fibrillation without feeling it. Elective cardioversion without anticoagulation or transesophageal imaging is not advised once the arrhythmia has lasted longer than 48 hours in the US guideline or 24 hours in the 2024 ESC guideline [UNVERIFIED: thresholds from memory, check both guidelines]. If he deteriorates and must be shocked, shock him: sedate, synchronize, start at a high biphasic energy, and anticoagulate.

Anticoagulation

Count the risk out loud. Heart failure (new today), hypertension, age 65 to 74 and diabetes give him at least 4 points on CHA2DS2-VASc, or on the sex-neutral CHA2DS2-VA used by the 2024 ESC guideline. Vascular disease would add one, depending on how his coronary plaque is classified. That is well above the threshold for anticoagulation. He has no bleeding history and takes no antiplatelet drug. Assess bleeding risk to find what can be corrected, not as a reason to withhold.

A direct oral anticoagulant is what he will go home on. While he is on a mask and may still need a tube, a heparin infusion is often more practical because it can be stopped. Enoxaparin is a reasonable bridge. The choice should be stated, not defaulted.

Disposition

Choose by what the receiving unit must be able to do: continue positive pressure, monitor continuously, reassess often over the next few hours, and intubate without moving him if the mask fails. In most hospitals that is an intensive care or coronary care bed. A step-down unit that can do all of that is an equally sound answer. A general ward is not, while he is on a mask.

Pitfalls

  • Treating the rate and missing the heart failure.
  • Continuing a calcium channel blocker after the ultrasound shows a weak ventricle.
  • Deferring positive pressure because the monitor's rate is louder than the saturation.
  • A fluid bolus for tachycardia in a patient with wet lungs.
  • Cardioverting a rhythm of unknown duration without anticoagulation.
  • Calling a rate of 108 a success while the breathing has not changed.
