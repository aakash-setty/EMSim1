# EMSim case design guide

Oct 5, 2026 · @Aakash

EMSim gives a junior doctor the experience of being the one who decides, safely and as often as they want. This guide holds the beliefs every case must honor. It prescribes no template. Two cases that follow it faithfully should look nothing alike.

It is written for an AI that chooses and writes cases, and for the physicians who direct it. Our learners are PGY1 and PGY2 emergency medicine residents and fourth-year medical students.

## The one idea

**You are the doctor, and the patient responds to you.**

A textbook tells you what to do. A lecture tells you why. Only practice gives you the weight of deciding and the sight of what your decision did. Real practice is rationed, supervised and unrepeatable. A simulated patient can be met alone, at any hour, and met again. Every choice in case design serves that one experience.

## What we believe

**1. The learner decides.** The case never plays itself, never narrows the choice to a menu of three, and never shows the answer before the decision. If the learner did not have to think, nothing was learned.

**2. The patient is the teacher.** The first feedback is physiology: a number moves, a face changes, a breath eases. Words come second. A consequence the learner watches teaches more than a verdict the learner reads.

**3. Difficulty belongs to the medicine.** A hard case is a hard patient. It is never a hard program, a long read, a hidden control or a trick of wording. Whatever is difficult should be the thing a doctor finds difficult.

**4. Failure is safe, visible and owned.** A mistake shows what it did, can be traced to a decision the learner made, and can be recovered from. Nothing is scored that the learner could not have reasoned out. The case can always be played again, and the second play can go differently.

**5. Truth comes first.** Feedback is never false. Evidence is stated at its real strength. Where good doctors disagree, the case says so and accepts both. Time is compressed openly, never secretly.

**6. The learner is a colleague.** We respect their time and intelligence. No lecture before they have acted, no scolding after, no padding anywhere. The voice is one doctor talking to another.

**7. Every case is its own thing.** Form follows the medicine. The structure of a case is discovered in the clinical problem, never chosen from a menu. A library of cases built on one pattern teaches the pattern, and learners stop reading the patient.

**8. A case leaves something that travels.** Beyond the diagnosis, each case carries one idea the learner will use on a different patient next month.

## Hard without frustrating

This is the standard every case is held to. Struggle and friction feel alike in the moment and are opposites in effect. Struggle is the learner wrestling with the medicine. Friction is the learner wrestling with us.

| Struggle we want | Friction we remove |
| --- | --- |
| Not knowing which problem to treat first | Not knowing what the controls can do |
| Choosing between two plausible treatments | Being penalized for a reasonable choice |
| Acting on incomplete information | Being denied information a real doctor could get |
| Seeing a patient worsen after a wrong call | A case that ends without saying why |
| Having to recover from a mistake | Feedback that is false, vague or scolding |
| Running out of time because of indecision | Running out of time because of reading or waiting |

**The test.** After a bad run, whom does the learner blame? If the answer is themselves, and they want another go, the case is hard. If the answer is the software, the case is frustrating. Nothing else about a case matters until it passes this test.

**When beliefs collide,** truth outranks clarity, clarity outranks pace, and pace outranks realism.

## Learning goals come first

A case exists to teach a few things. Decide what they are before anything else, because they decide everything else: which patient, which findings, which states, which mistakes are possible, and what the debrief says.

**Start with what a competent doctor must do for this condition.** Before writing any goal, list the core competencies of managing the condition in the emergency department: what must be recognized, what must be done and in what order, what must be avoided, and when to call for help. Goals are chosen from that list. They are not invented from what would make an interesting case.

**Build the list from the field.** Each source answers a different question.

- **Current guidelines and consensus statements** say what is right for the patient.
- **EM training standards** say what a resident is expected to do alone. The ABEM Model of the Clinical Practice of Emergency Medicine and the ACGME EM Milestones are the usual references.
- **Published simulation cases on the same condition**, where they exist, say what other educators judged essential. Read their objectives and critical actions. MedEdPORTAL, JETem and EM Sim Cases are places to look.
- **Established EM education sites** say where learners go wrong in practice.
- **The directing physician** settles disagreements and fills gaps.

When several sources name the same action as critical, treat it as core. When no published simulation exists, say so and lean on the guideline and the physician. Published cases are mostly written for a team around a manikin, so some of their objectives are about teamwork or procedures and will not fit a single learner at a screen. Take the clinical decisions and leave the rest.

Write down where each goal came from. A goal with no source is an opinion, and the case notes should say so.

**From that list, keep the goals that are:**

- **Core.** It is on the competency list, and it changes what happens to patients. A junior who learns it will practice differently.
- **Useful beyond this patient.** It names a way of thinking, and the diagnosis is the example. A goal about how a second condition changes the safe treatment will matter again. A goal that names one drug for one case will not.
- **Something a learner can do.** A decision or an action the case can see. "Understands" and "appreciates" cannot be seen.
- **Hard for a real reason.** There is a tempting wrong answer, or a habit that fails here.
- **Few.** Each goal needs room in the case. A case with ten goals teaches none of them.

**Then let each goal earn its place in the structure.** For every goal, ask what the case must contain so that the learner meets it by deciding, not by being told.

| If the goal is | The case needs |
| --- | --- |
| A finding changes the right treatment | A way to look for the finding, and a patient who responds differently before and after it is known |
| One problem must be treated before another | A patient with both, who visibly does better in the right order |
| A common habit is wrong for this patient | The habit available and tempting, and a patient who shows what it does |
| Knowing when to stop | A treatment that helps at first and harms if continued |

These are illustrations of the reasoning. They are not a list to choose from.

**The test.** Take any state, finding or branch in the case and ask which goal it serves. If the answer is none, it is decoration, and it costs the learner attention. Take any goal and ask where the learner has to act on it. If the answer is nowhere, or only in the debrief, the case does not teach it yet.

## Choosing a case

A case is worth building when the honest answer to each of these is yes. They are questions to think with, not boxes to tick.

- **Is there a real decision?** Something a thoughtful junior could get wrong for a good reason.
- **Can the patient show the difference?** The right and wrong choices must lead to patients the learner can tell apart.
- **Is it worth carrying?** The lesson should matter for many patients, or for one that cannot be missed.
- **Is it rarely practiced alone?** The best cases hand the learner a decision a senior usually makes for them.
- **Does it add something the library lacks?**

**Variety is a requirement.** Before building, say how this case differs from every case we have. Cases can differ in many ways, and a healthy library uses all of them:

- **Tempo.** Seconds, minutes, or no clock at all.
- **What is uncertain.** The diagnosis, the treatment, the timing, when to stop, or whether to act.
- **The kind of right answer.** Do more, do less, wait and watch, ask someone, or send the patient home.
- **Where the danger hides.** Obvious at the door, behind normal vitals, or created by the treatment.
- **The patient and the room.** Age, story, setting, who is at the bedside, what resources exist.
- **How it feels.** Urgent, puzzling, tender, tense.

A case that matches an existing one on most of these needs a reason to exist.

## Structure and states

This is the hardest part of writing a case, and the part most damaged by a formula. We offer a way of thinking and no shapes to copy.

**Start from the learning goals.** The structure is whatever makes the learner act on each goal. The stories, states and links below are how you find it.

**Tell the true stories first.** Before any structure, write what happens to this patient when treated well, when treated in each plausible wrong way, and when left alone. Write them as a clinician would tell them at the bedside. The structure of the case is already inside those stories.

**A state is a moment that changes what a good doctor would do.** Find the points in the stories where the patient becomes different enough that the next right move changes. Those are the states. If the learner cannot perceive the difference, or the difference does not change the next move, it is not a state.

**Every state asks a question.** "What now?" must have a real answer in each one, and a different answer from the state before.

**Every link is a claim about cause.** Moving from one state to another says "this is what that does to this patient". A clinician should agree with every link. If a link exists to make the case work and not because it is true, remove it.

**Use as few states as the truth allows, and as many as it needs.** Some problems are a single long decision. Some branch widely. Some never deteriorate. Do not add a crisis because cases usually have one, and do not cut a real branch to stay tidy.

**The learner is never stranded.** From anywhere, there is something meaningful to do, and something happens if they do nothing.

**Ask these of any web before writing content.**

- Can I tell each path as a short story a clinician would recognize?
- Is there a place where nothing the learner does matters?
- Is there a place where one choice ends everything and teaches nothing?
- Would a second play feel different from the first?
- Does this web look like one we have already built? If so, is that the medicine, or habit?

## How we teach

These are the methods we bet on. Some rest on evidence. Some go beyond it, and we say which. Going beyond the evidence is allowed. Pretending it is evidence is not.

| Method | What it means here | Standing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Act first, explain after | The learner meets the problem before being taught the answer | Supported in 53 studies, mostly school math and science (Sinha and Kapur 2021) |
| Feedback at once and specific | The patient responds in seconds, and the debrief names the decision | The best-supported feature of simulation (Cook 2013) |
| Practice again, and again later | Replay now, return next week | Repetition and spaced practice improved skills (Cook 2013) |
| The same idea in different patients | One principle met across varied cases | Clinical variation improved skills (Cook 2013) |
| Recall over review | Decide from memory, do not recognize from a list | Well established in learning research. From memory, not checked |
| Mastery, not a grade | Play until fluent. A score is a signpost, never the point | Supported for procedural skills. From memory, not checked |
| Substance over realism | Effort goes to decisions and consequences | High fidelity added "modest or limited benefit" in 18 studies (Norman 2012) |
| Safety to fail | No shame, no audience, no record held against the learner | Our bet. Widely held in simulation teaching, not checked |
| Story and curiosity | A patient worth caring about, a question worth answering | Our bet |

One limit applies to all of it. No study shows that a screen-based case like ours changes what a doctor does with a real patient. We believe it can. We have not shown it.

## What we refuse

- A quiz dressed as a patient.
- A trap the learner could not have seen.
- Feedback that is false, even when the truth is harder to build.
- A lecture before the learner has acted.
- Difficulty made from confusion.
- Realism for its own sake.
- A template.
